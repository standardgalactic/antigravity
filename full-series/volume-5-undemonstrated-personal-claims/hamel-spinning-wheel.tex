\begin{abstract}
David Hamel, a Canadian carpenter, claimed from the late 1970s that he had received instructions from extraterrestrial contact experiences to build a spinning-magnet gravity-manipulation device, and subsequently constructed several large disc-shaped assemblies of rotating magnets and generators he described as antigravity spacecraft components, none of which has ever been demonstrated to lift its own weight, generate net electrical power, or exhibit any measured departure from ordinary electromagnetic behavior under conditions witnessed and verified by anyone outside Hamel's own circle of associates. This paper treats the case as a useful boundary example among the claims surveyed in this series, one whose origin story explicitly rests on personal spiritual or contact experience rather than any purported experimental anomaly, and considers what distinguishes an unfalsifiable revelation-based claim of this kind from the more common pattern of a claimed anomalous measurement later found to have a mundane cause.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

David Hamel described experiences beginning in 1975 in which he stated he was taken aboard an extraterrestrial craft and shown, or otherwise came to understand, the operating principles of a gravity-manipulating propulsion device, which he subsequently attempted to construct using arrangements of rotating permanent magnets, generators, and a spinning outer disc structure, building several successive versions over subsequent decades and demonstrating them to visitors, documentary filmmakers, and a small community of supporters, without any version producing a measured or independently verified lift, thrust, or anomalous energy output.

\section{Why This Case Differs Structurally From Most Others in This Series}

Most devices surveyed elsewhere in this series originate from a claimed laboratory measurement or engineering observation, an apparent weight change, an unexpected voltage reading, a device that seemed to move without a visible cause, later found to have a conventional physical explanation. Hamel's device instead originates explicitly from a personal, subjectively reported contact experience presented as the source of the device's design principles, a category of claim that is not, in the same sense, a misinterpreted measurement waiting for a mundane physical explanation, since no measurement or anomalous observation is the claim's evidentiary starting point at all; the starting point is testimony about a personal experience, and the device's construction is an attempt to physically realize what that testimony describes rather than an attempt to explain an already-observed anomaly.

\section{What This Means for How the Claim Should Be Evaluated}

A revelation-based origin story does not, by itself, make a subsequently built device's physical behavior unevaluable; if Hamel's constructed spinning wheel produced measurable lift or anomalous energy output, this would be worth investigating on its own physical merits regardless of how the design was originally conceived, in the same way a chemist's dream-derived insight into a molecular structure, a genuinely documented historical occurrence in organic chemistry, does not need to be dismissed merely because of its origin. What distinguishes Hamel's case, and the reason it is treated here rather than set aside as merely a spiritual claim outside this series's scope, is that in over four decades since 1975, across several successively built versions of the device, no measured lift, no measured net energy output, and no independent replication under conditions ruling out mundane explanations, including simple visual illusion created by a rotating outer shell obscuring a stationary inner support structure, a specific critique raised by several who have examined available footage of the demonstrations, has ever been documented.

\section{Why the Absence of Any Positive Measurement Is the Load-Bearing Fact Here}

Because the claim's origin is not itself a physical measurement, the entire evidentiary weight for the device's function rests on its constructed behavior, and the complete absence of any positive, independently verified measurement across a four-decade construction history, longer than most other devices surveyed in this series have existed at all, is considerably more informative here than it would be for a device with at least some initially ambiguous measurement to explain away, since there is no ambiguous measurement in Hamel's case requiring any explanation, mundane or otherwise; there is simply no demonstrated physical effect to be explained.

\section{The Entropic Gravity Framing}

Insofar as Hamel's device claims to manipulate gravity through spinning magnet configurations, the same absence-of-coupling-channel objection raised against the Searl Effect Generator and other magnet-based antigravity claims elsewhere in this series applies here in a comparatively unambiguous form, since a magnetic field, static or rotating, has no established channel into the large-scale ordered-configuration redistribution that produces gravitational curvature under either the standard or the entropic account, a mechanism-level objection that would apply even if the device's construction and demonstration history were more evidentially ambiguous than it actually is.

\section{Objections}

Supporters point to the device's decades of continued construction and Hamel's evident personal sincerity and consistency in describing his experiences as evidence against a simple hoax interpretation. Sincerity and consistency in describing a personal experience are not, on their own, evidence for the physical function of a device built in response to that experience, and this paper's argument does not require characterizing Hamel's testimony as insincere in order to note that the device itself, evaluated purely on its constructed physical behavior across four decades, has not produced any measured effect requiring explanation, mundane or otherwise.

\section{Conclusion}

David Hamel's spinning wheel device, unlike most other claims surveyed in this series, originates from a personal contact experience rather than an ambiguous measurement, and its evaluation therefore rests entirely on whether the constructed device has ever demonstrated a measured physical effect, which across four decades and multiple constructed versions it has not, leaving the claim without either a positive measurement to explain or a specified mechanism by which spinning magnets could couple to gravitational curvature under any account of gravitation currently available.
